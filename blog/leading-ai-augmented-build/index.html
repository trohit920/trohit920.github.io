Leading an AI-augmented build

Rohit Thakur · 20 November 2026 · 6 min read

Kursi Kushti is a free satirical fighting game that runs in the browser. Coding agents wrote most of its code. My part was the brief and its later changes of direction, the gates every change had to pass (taken from my own working rules), a requirement that a separate agent review every change and that disputes be settled by measurement, the decisions only the owner could make, and the final check on a real phone. Reviewer agents did the reviews and agents took the measurements. This post is about how that setup worked, because it is the part I can honestly claim.

The question

What does leading a build look like when most of the code is written by agents? My answer after this project: much like leading people, with less tolerance for vague instructions. An agent does what the brief says, quickly and confidently, so the brief and the pass condition carry most of the weight.

The setup

  • The product: a 2.5D brawler in TypeScript and three.js, with no server. The rules live in a deterministic core: 60 ticks per second, integer state, a seeded random number generator.
  • The team: one written plan split into numbered tasks, an implementer agent per task, a separate reviewer agent, and me as the owner, who saw a demo at the end of each phase and made the calls nobody else should make.
  • The timeline: the plan and the first commit landed on 6 November; the game went live on 11 November; the last of 110 commits on main is from 13 November.
  • The fighters: 12 of them are 3D models made from commissioned caricature drawings with an image-to-3D model, run locally on a laptop GPU with 6 GB of memory.

The game is live at kursikushti.pages.dev. The repository is private; the numbers below come from its notes, tests and build output.

The gates

Every task in the plan ended with the same gate: typecheck, lint and the full test suite pass before a commit. Each phase added a browser check against a written script (load a match, press keys, confirm health drops, save a screenshot) and a demo to me. Later the same gate moved into deployment: a push to main deploys to Cloudflare Pages only after the check and the production build pass.

When I re-ran the suite for this post it reported 3,852 passing tests in 138 files. That number deserves a caveat: 1,778 of them cover the simulation core, and everything else covers rendering, input, menus, app wiring, audio, the build scripts and the avatar tool. A large count is easy for agents to produce. What mattered more was tests that encode a decision. One review found that the signature combos could not land in open space. The fix had a written pass condition (all four combos land from every gap at which the opener connects, in both directions), and the chosen constant was the smallest value that met it. Another: a live match ended in 37 seconds, so the plan gained a test that AI-versus-AI rounds have a median length between 25 and 50 seconds.

Story one: choosing a model under real constraints

The fighters needed to become 3D models without a cloud service, on a 6 GB GPU, under a licence that allows use from South Korea, where I live, and commercial use later. Those three constraints removed most of the field before any quality test.

ModelOutcome
Hunyuan3DRuled out on licence: it excludes South Korea
TRELLISRuled out: too much GPU memory, and its texture step uses a non-commercial rasteriser
SPAR3DTested: too much GPU memory to run well on 6 GB, and flatter meshes on these drawings
SF3DChosen: about 6 GB, under a minute per fighter, free under US$1M annual revenue

The licence check came first: reading a licence is cheaper than tuning a model you cannot ship. SF3D's own obligations were written into the pipeline notes and shipped (the "Powered by Stability AI" credit line on the About screen, and a NOTICE file).

One variable at a time

The first meshes were flat cut-outs with a faint mirrored face on the back of the head. Instead of swapping models again, the next step was an experiment on one fighter that changed one thing per run: the same cut-out with a transparent background, the same cut-out on white, and a three-quarter view. On white, SF3D produced a round head with hair on the back. A plausible reason is that SF3D removes the background itself, as it does in training, so a white background is closer to what it expects. Nine of the 12 fighters were regenerated on white. The other three kept their earlier builds: one lost its legs on white, one stayed flat either way, and one was the pilot build.

Story two: floating fighters, measured instead of guessed

After the rounder models went in, some fighters looked as if they hovered above the floor. The easy guesses were the model, the normalisation step or the camera. Instead, a diagnostic page posed each skinned mesh and recorded its lowest point.

The measurement found the cause quickly. The rig set a minimum pelvis height of half the model's depth in every pose. That clearance was meant to stop a knocked-down body sinking through the floor, but it was applied while standing too. The rounder models were deeper, so they rose 0.26 to 0.55 units in the fighting stance. The fix scales the clearance with how far the body tilts: zero upright, half the depth lying flat. The change is a one-line function, with tests for upright, flat and in between.

The last row stays in on purpose: one toe still dips below the -0.15 band we set. A report saying "every fighter stands on the floor" would read better and be slightly wrong.

What I would keep

  • A rulings log. 55 decisions were written down with the reason and the cost if wrong, such as "specials easier to dodge; tune after playtest". It made reviewing a week's work possible.
  • Separate maker and checker. On one start-up timing, the implementer measured 770 ms slower and the reviewer's measurement was within noise. Neither was taken on trust; it was accepted for now and marked for measurement on a real phone.
  • Human gates where only a human can judge. The real-phone check was mine: the gate closed only when the live build worked on my iPhone. Bots could prove balance numbers, not whether the difficulty ladder feels fair to a person.
  • Content rules in the plan and in tests. The plan forbids flags, symbols and religious markers on any costume and fixes the disclaimer text. Tests check the game's copy against content rules and hold all caricatures to one skin value and to shared ranges for facial features and body proportions.

What I would do differently

  • Write the hard constraints before the first prototype. Licence, GPU memory and content limits decided more than any benchmark did. Some of the fighters' look work was redone when later rules changed it.
  • Track numbers in one place. The test count drifted across hand-off notes (3,686, then 3,753, now 3,852) and an older write-up still says "about 1.5 MB" for a site whose build is about 3.6 MB with all 12 models. A generated stats file would have stopped that.
  • Review generated content earlier. The English and Hindi trailers are made from captured gameplay, but the Hindi script was written by an agent and nobody, me included, has yet proofread it line by line; its current voice is licensed for non-commercial use only.

Limits

  • I did not write most of this code. What I can claim is the direction, the gates and the owner decisions in the notes; most rulings were the agents', recorded for me to review. That is a different claim from having typed it.
  • One week, one owner. A solo project run like a small team is not evidence about how a team of people should adopt agents.
  • Tests are not playtests. The suite proves the rules are deterministic and the decisions hold. Whether the game is fun is a separate question that only players answer.
